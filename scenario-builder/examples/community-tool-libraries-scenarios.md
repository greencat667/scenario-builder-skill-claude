# The question

Our trend report on community tool libraries concluded that the funding and policy window is open now, and that we should consider acting before it narrows. Before committing a decade of programme effort, we want to test that decision against several futures, not just the one we'd like.

**Focal question:** Should Riverside Environmental Trust make a network of community tool libraries its main local programme for 2026–2036?

The answer depends on things we don't control: who pays for local services after English local government is reorganised, and whether community groups or commercial platforms end up running everyday access to shared things. A single forecast here would be false precision.

**Scenarios are not predictions.** None of these worlds is labelled "most likely". Their job is to test our choices against several futures, so we're not caught out by the one we didn't picture.

# The four worlds at a glance

The two axes are **who pays** for everyday lending, and **who runs** it.

|  | **Community-run** | **Platform-run** |
|---|---|---|
| **Public purse** | **The Civic Shed** — councils fund lending as local infrastructure, delivered through community trusts | **Borrow-as-a-Service** — councils buy a slick lending service from a national operator, like bike hire |
| **User's pocket** | **Members' Mutual** — no public money; member-owned co-ops thrive where people can pay, and vanish elsewhere | **Rent Anything** — borrowing goes mainstream through commercial rental and delivery, with no charity involved |

**Axis A — Who pays:** *public purse* (councils and public bodies fund lending as local infrastructure, from climate, waste or development budgets) ↔ *user's pocket* (schemes must pay their own way from fees, memberships and sponsors). This is genuinely open. Councils are already funding libraries of things from climate budgets ([Cooperative City](https://cooperativecity.org/inside-the-library-of-things-uk/)), but 35 councils needed about £1.5bn in exceptional financial support to balance their books for 2026–27 ([Local Government Lawyer](https://www.localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/governance/396-governance-news/99797-government-confirms-35-councils-to-receive-1-5bn-in-exceptional-financial-support)). Tight money doesn't settle the question: it forces councils to choose, and lending could land on either side of that choice.

**Axis B — Who runs it:** *community-run* (local, member-governed hubs; repair and advice alongside lending) ↔ *platform-run* (national commercial or quasi-commercial operators; apps, lockers and delivery). Also open. Community schemes are growing, with Ethical Consumer's UK directory now listing "over 100" libraries of things ([Ethical Consumer](https://www.ethicalconsumer.org/home-garden/library-things-directory)). But commercial peer-to-peer rental is consolidating and cutting its fees ([Hygglo](https://hygglo.com/uk/fatllama)), and national hire firms already offer free delivery on hundreds of items ([HSS](https://www.hss.com/hire/p/p02344/18v-cordless-combi-drill)).

# What's true in every world

Some forces are high-impact but not very uncertain. Whatever happens on the two axes, we can plan on these.

- **Money, not green values, drives most borrowing.** In the UK, 79% of people buying second-hand say saving money is a motive, against 47% who cite the environment ([eBay](https://www.ebayinc.com/stories/press-room/uk/uk-leads-global-shift-to-pre-loved-shopping-new-ebay-data-reveals/)). Research on sharing schemes found saving money predicted taking part better than environmental attitudes did ([Hamari et al.](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/the-sharing-economy-why-people-participate-in-collaborative-consu)). Any model that relies on people borrowing *because it's green* will struggle in every world.
- **A large, young renting population in small homes.** 19% of English households rent privately, and 69% of 16–24-year-old householders do. That share has barely moved in a decade ([English Housing Survey](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/chapters-for-english-housing-survey-2024-to-2025-headline-findings-on-demographics-and-household-resilience/chapter-1-profile-of-households-and-dwellings)). Private rented homes average 76m² against 110m² for owner-occupied homes ([EHS headline report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6930595d4bedc0e762303ffa/2024-25_EHS_Headline_Report.pdf)).
- **A quarter of households have no car**, rising to about 44% of the lowest-income fifth ([National Travel Survey](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/national-travel-survey-2025/nts-2025-household-car-availability-and-trends-in-car-trips)). Carrying a carpet cleaner home is a real barrier for millions of people.
- **The councils we work with will change.** Decisions in July 2026 merge 134 councils into 38 unitary authorities, with the new councils taking over in April 2028 ([Hansard written statement](https://www.theyworkforyou.com/wms/?id=2026-07-16.hcws286.h)). Relationships built today with a district council may not survive the transition.
- **Formal volunteering won't bounce back on its own.** Monthly formal volunteering in England fell from 23% of adults in 2019–20 to 16% in 2023–24 ([Civil Society](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/volunteering-rates-still-failing-to-reach-pre-pandemic-levels-government-reports.html)). No model can assume a rota of unpaid people keeps growing.

# World 1 — The Civic Shed

*Councils fund lending as local infrastructure, delivered through community trusts.*

## How we got here

The turning point came with reorganisation. When the new unitary councils took over in April 2028, most inherited a patchwork of district-funded schemes: a library of things here, a repair café grant there. Several chose to rationalise them into a single "borrow and repair" service, specified like any other council service and funded from climate and waste budgets. Diverting even a small share of bulky waste away from collection saved money the council was already spending.

Money was still tight, so councils didn't run the service themselves. They commissioned local trusts and charities to deliver it under five-year agreements. Crucially, those agreements ran longer than the old annual grants.

The new community right of first refusal on Assets of Community Value ([Local Government Lawyer](https://www.localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/governance/314-governance-a-risk-articles/100441-the-english-devolution-and-community-empowerment-act-2026)) gave trusts a route into closing library branches and empty shop units. By 2031, lending hubs sat in about half the library buildings that would otherwise have shut. By 2036 a council-funded tool library is as unremarkable as a council-funded swimming pool, and just as often argued about at budget time.

## What's true in this world

- Most towns have a lending and repair hub, usually in a library, community centre or former shop
- Hubs run on five-year council service agreements, with performance targets on usage and waste diverted
- Paid hub coordinators are normal; volunteers help with repair and events, not the basics
- Booking uses a shared regional system specified by the council
- Membership is free or means-tested, so low-income households use it more than in any other world
- Small volunteer schemes that couldn't meet contract requirements merged into larger trusts or closed

## Winners and losers

- **Doing well:** established local charities with delivery capacity; residents without cars or storage space; councils that can report waste savings
- **Squeezed:** small volunteer-run schemes; commercial hire firms in towns with good hubs; areas whose council said no; trusts that grew dependent on a single contract

## A moment in this world

Jo reads the contract renewal email twice on the bus. Five more years, same budget, plus a new target: 15% of members from the council's housing waiting list. She's not sure how they'll measure it.

At the hub, the old reference section is now shelving: ladders, carpet cleaners, a row of sewing machines. Kwame is fixing a strimmer at the repair bench while a teenager on work experience watches. The queue is short because it's raining.

A councillor visits at eleven with a photographer. He asks whether they could "badge it more clearly as a council service". Jo says the members like that it's theirs. He smiles and says they'll talk about it at the review. She makes a note to find out who chairs the review.

## What it means for us

- The biggest opportunity: become a delivery partner for a council-wide service, with multi-year income
- The biggest threat: we become a contractor, measured on council targets, with our advocacy voice constrained
- Reorganisation is the moment of maximum influence. The new councils' first plans get written in 2027–28
- Capability we'd wish we'd built: contract management, and data that proves waste and cost savings

# World 2 — Borrow-as-a-Service

*Councils buy a slick lending service from a national operator, like bike hire.*

## How we got here

Councils did decide lending was worth paying for, but they bought it the way they buy bike hire: from an operator. By 2027 a handful of national platforms were offering councils a complete package of lockers, an app, insurance and maintenance. The councils paid a fee per locker and set the prices residents paid.

Squeezed for staff time, new unitary councils found one contract with one supplier easier than a dozen grants to a dozen charities. The first county-wide lending contract went to a national operator in 2029, and others copied it.

The service was genuinely good. Lockers appeared in supermarket car parks, transport hubs and blocks of flats, and borrowing rose steeply. But the operators optimised for high-turnover items and simple returns. Repair, advice and the community side of lending mostly fell away, except where a local charity negotiated a small "community partner" role.

## What's true in this world

- Lockers are everywhere in towns and cities, open 24/7 and bookable in seconds
- A small number of national operators run most council lending contracts
- Items are standardised, robust and replaced on a schedule, not repaired locally
- Usage data belongs to the operator; councils get dashboards
- Charities act as outreach or "community partners" at best, on small sub-contracts
- Rural areas and low-density estates get fewer lockers, because contracts reward usage

## Winners and losers

- **Doing well:** platform operators; busy, digitally confident residents; councils that value simple procurement
- **Squeezed:** local charities that ran hubs; repair volunteers; people without smartphones or bank cards; rural villages

## A moment in this world

The locker by the tram stop flashes green and releases a carpet cleaner, still warm from the last user. Danielle has booked it for four hours, charged to her card. It's cheaper than last year.

She doesn't know who owns the locker. The app has a council logo on the splash screen and a different company's name in the small print.

The spray nozzle is cracked. She taps "report a fault" and gets a £2 credit. Back home, she remembers the old community workshop two streets over: it closed after its grant ran out. She'd once watched someone there fix a vacuum with a paperclip and a lot of swearing. The new service doesn't fix anything. It just swaps the broken ones out on Thursdays.

## What it means for us

- Borrowing succeeds at scale, but our role in providing it shrinks to outreach and advocacy
- There's a real gap in reaching people platforms under-serve: no smartphone, no card, no locker nearby
- Repair and durability become our distinctive contribution. The platform replaces; we can champion fixing
- Capability we'd wish we'd built: the ability to negotiate as a community partner in procurement, and to campaign on contract terms such as repair, data and access

# World 3 — Members' Mutual

*No public money; member-owned co-ops thrive where people can pay, and vanish elsewhere.*

## How we got here

The council money dried up first. Exceptional financial support spread from dozens of councils to many more, and discretionary climate budgets were among the first lines cut. Reorganisation made it worse: new unitary councils spent their first years merging systems, and grants to small schemes quietly stopped.

The schemes that survived turned themselves into member-owned co-operatives. They charged an annual membership, borrowed against community shares, and ran on fees and the occasional local business sponsor. It worked where there were enough people who could pay £60 a year and wanted to belong to something. Neighbourliness held up better than trust in institutions; belonging to a neighbourhood stayed common even as neighbour trust slipped ([Community and Engagement Survey](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/community-and-engagement-survey-202526-neighbourhood/community-and-engagement-survey-202526-neighbourhood-and-community-report)).

By 2036 the map is patchy. There are thriving mutuals in university towns and gentrifying inner suburbs, and almost nothing in the estates and former industrial towns that would have gained most.

## What's true in this world

- Libraries of things exist as independent co-ops, charging membership plus small loan fees
- The strongest have hundreds of members, a paid coordinator, and a waiting list for popular items
- There's no public funding, so no targets, no council reporting, and no council protection either
- Most members are homeowners, graduates and already "sharing-minded"
- Deprived areas and small towns have few or no schemes
- Co-ops share know-how through informal networks, running on goodwill

## Winners and losers

- **Doing well:** well-organised, better-off neighbourhoods; co-operative and community-share infrastructure; the most entrepreneurial scheme founders
- **Squeezed:** low-income households, renters who move often, car-free households outside dense areas, and any charity that relied on council grants

## A moment in this world

The AGM runs over, as usual. Forty members squeezed into the back of the café, voting on whether to raise the membership to £72. Priyanka argues for a "pay what you can" tier. Someone asks, reasonably, who pays for it.

It passes, narrowly, capped at thirty places. Afterwards Tom, the coordinator, stacks chairs and checks the numbers. The co-op is solvent. Three new members this week, all from the new flats by the station.

On the bus home he passes through the Ashgrove estate, where the old estate scheme closed in 2029. Nobody ever restarted it. He thinks about the thirty places, and how many people on the estate would even know to ask.

## What it means for us

- Our grant-making and back-office role becomes more valuable, because mutuals need insurance, systems and advice they can't afford alone
- The equity gap becomes the central problem, and the natural focus for a charity like us
- We can't count on any council partnership; we need our own income or a charitable funder willing to subsidise access
- Capability we'd wish we'd built: community-share and co-op expertise, and a cross-subsidy model that funds places in poorer areas

# World 4 — Rent Anything

*Borrowing goes mainstream through commercial rental and delivery, with no charity involved.*

## How we got here

The charity sector's framing was that sharing needed support. In the end, sharing didn't need charities at all. Peer-to-peer rental platforms consolidated, cut their fees ([Hygglo](https://hygglo.com/uk/fatllama)) and added insurance and next-day delivery. National hire firms, already delivering free on hundreds of items ([HSS](https://www.hss.com/hire/p/p02344/18v-cordless-combi-drill)), pushed into consumer rental. Around 2030, a big DIY retailer launched "rent it, don't buy it" at the till.

Second-hand buying had already gone mainstream, with nearly half of UK consumers buying pre-loved at least monthly ([eBay](https://www.ebayinc.com/stories/press-room/uk/uk-leads-global-shift-to-pre-loved-shopping-new-ebay-data-reveals/)). Renting was a short step further.

Council money for community schemes shrank, and most small libraries of things couldn't match the convenience of an app. Some closed; some became repair cafés. By 2036, renting a pressure washer is as ordinary as ordering a takeaway. The environmental story is mixed: fewer tools bought, but more vans on the road and cheaper, less durable kit.

## What's true in this world

- Renting occasional-use things is normal and easy, through apps with delivery and collection
- A few large platforms and retailers dominate; prices are low and change with demand
- Very few community lending schemes remain; repair cafés survive as hobby and social spaces
- Rental firms buy robust kit and replace it rather than repair it
- People without bank cards, credit history or a fixed address struggle to rent
- There is little local ownership, and little say for users in what's stocked or how it's run

## Winners and losers

- **Doing well:** platforms and retailers; convenience-seeking households with money and phones; manufacturers selling to rental fleets
- **Squeezed:** community schemes and the charities that funded them; people who are unbanked or have poor credit; neighbourhoods valued for local, face-to-face exchange

## A moment in this world

Lewis rents a tile cutter from his phone at 10pm. It arrives at 8 the next morning in a van with three other rentals for the same street. The driver doesn't have time to say what it is.

The bathroom goes well. He posts a photo in the group chat. His sister, who's between flats and has no fixed address on file, can't open an account on the same app. She asks if he'll rent one for her. He says sure, but the platform's terms say the renter has to be the user. They both know nobody checks.

On Saturday he walks past the old library of things. It's a repair café now, twice a month. There's a hand-written sign: "We fix what they won't." He takes a photo of that too.

## What it means for us

- Our core argument, that borrowing beats buying, wins, but without us
- Running hubs directly would be a poor use of our money in this world; the market does the lending
- Our leverage shifts to rules: product durability, repair rights, rental-fleet standards, and access for people the market excludes
- Capability we'd wish we'd built: policy and campaigning expertise on repair and product regulation, and alliances with consumer groups

# Implications and robust moves

## No-regret moves

1. **Reach the people other models miss: renters, car-free households and people on low incomes.** In The Civic Shed, councils will pay for inclusion targets. In Borrow-as-a-Service, it's the gap the platform leaves. In Members' Mutual, it's the equity problem at the heart of that world. In Rent Anything, it's who the market excludes. Weakest in: Rent Anything, where we'd reach them through advocacy and partners rather than our own hubs. Still worth it. *First step: test whether doorstep delivery works for renters without cars (see the experiment cards).*
2. **Build shared back-office services: insurance, booking, safety checks and training that several hubs can use.** This lowers costs for community schemes in three worlds and makes us a credible partner in the fourth. Weakest in: Rent Anything, where there are fewer hubs to serve, but the cost is modest.
3. **Collect good evidence on usage, who's reached, purchases avoided and waste diverted.** Councils need it to commission, members need it to renew, and campaigners need it to argue for rules. It helps in every world.
4. **Keep repair skills alive.** Repair is the one thing platforms don't do and councils value for waste reasons. It survives in all four worlds, even as a café in Rent Anything.
5. **Campaign for durability and repair rules.** Great Britain's current spare-parts rules mainly benefit professional repairers ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2021/745/schedule/19/crossheading/design-for-repair-and-reuse-availability-of-spare-parts/made)). Better rules help in every world, and are our main lever in Rent Anything.

## Hedges

- **Keep our booking data and systems portable,** and avoid lock-in to any single platform. This protects against Borrow-as-a-Service.
- **Start a small members' income stream now,** even if it's only a fraction of costs. This protects against council money disappearing (Members' Mutual).
- **Keep our advocacy voice independent** of any council contract, with its own funding. This protects against The Civic Shed turning us into a contractor.

## Options to keep open

- **Whether to lease or buy premises.** Wait for the new unitary council's first corporate plan (2028) and any ACV opportunities.
- **Whether to bid to run a council-wide service.** Wait until a council signals it will commission one.

## Big bets

- **Become the regional delivery partner for a council-funded network.** Pays off in The Civic Shed. Make the bet if a new unitary names lending in its first plan or invites bids.
- **Become the community and repair partner to a national platform.** Pays off in Borrow-as-a-Service. Make the bet if a council near us awards a platform contract.
- **Shift our main effort to repair and product-regulation campaigning.** Pays off in Rent Anything. Make the bet if the directory count falls and a major retailer launches rental.

## Stress test of current options

| Option | The Civic Shed | Borrow-as-a-Service | Members' Mutual | Rent Anything |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Run three hubs directly, on start-up grants** | ⚠️ Works if we win the contract; stranded if another trust does | ❌ Undercut by lockers | ⚠️ Viable only in better-off areas | ❌ Undercut by apps |
| **B. Be the enabler: back office, insurance, grants and training for many hubs** | ✅ Natural fit for a commissioned network | ⚠️ Smaller role as community partner | ✅ Exactly what mutuals need | ⚠️ Few hubs left to serve |
| **C. Advocacy only: campaign on repair, durability and access** | ⚠️ Misses the delivery opportunity | ✅ Shapes contract terms | ⚠️ Doesn't fix the equity gap | ✅ Our main lever |

**The weakest point** is Option A, which is close to the "main local programme" in our focal question. It only works well in one world, and even there it depends on winning a contract. **Option B, with some of C, holds up best across all four.**

That suggests a sharper answer to the focal question: make community lending a major programme, but as an **enabler and advocate**, not mainly as a hub operator. Run one hub directly as a testbed, and reach renters and car-free households as the first proof of what we add.

## B. Axis pairs considered

1. **Who pays × Who runs it** *(chosen)*. Independence: public funding could flow to community trusts or to platforms, and user-paid lending could be co-operative or commercial. All four combinations have real-world precedents: council-funded community schemes, council bike-hire contracts with operators, member co-ops, and commercial rental. Coverage: captures money and market structure. It leaves out repair regulation, which appears inside each world and in the indicators instead.
2. **Who pays × Who does the work (volunteers ↔ paid staff and automation)**. *Rejected.* The two are strongly correlated: public money largely determines whether hubs can pay staff. The "public purse + all-volunteer" and "user-pays + fully staffed" corners were hard to make plausible.
3. **Who runs it × Strength of UK repair and durability rules**. *Rejected.* It's independent and surprising, but repair rules affect our focal decision less than funding does, so it tested our choice less sharply. It could be a useful second set for a campaigns-focused exercise.

## C. Critique notes

- **Axis A was value-laden in the first draft.** It was framed as "strong council funding ↔ weak council funding", which produced an obvious good world and bad world. Reframed as *public purse ↔ user's pocket*, so that user-paid worlds carry real upsides (independence, member ownership, market convenience) as well as costs.
- **Members' Mutual was too rosy.** The first draft read as the "community wins" world. Added the equity cost: the map is patchy, and the neighbourhoods with most to gain have the fewest schemes.
- **Rent Anything was a dystopia.** Revised to show real gains: borrowing becomes normal and cheap, which is our own stated goal. The discomfort for us is that it happens without us, which is the useful challenge.
- **Indicators that fired everywhere were cut.** "Libraries of things directory keeps growing" fired in three worlds. It was replaced with indicators on *how* new schemes are funded and governed.
- **No-regret move 2 was tested against its weakest world** (Rent Anything) and kept, with a note that its value is smaller there.

## D. How this was produced

This scenario set was built with an AI-assisted scenario method, the 2×2 critical-uncertainties approach. It started from the driving forces in our earlier trend report and added fresh research to fill gaps. Every present-day fact is linked to its source. The four worlds are structured imagination, not forecasts. Riverside Environmental Trust and the people in the vignettes are fictional.
